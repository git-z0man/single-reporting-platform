EUVD page: chart mockups

Nineteen panels in two parts, the EUVD as a whole and the exploited vulnerabilities, drawn from the EUVD's real data as of 2026-10-07, using the same renderer that would draw them on the live page. Nothing here is invented. Each panel says what you see, where the data comes from, and what the chart cannot show. The final charts would regenerate daily with no manual step: the daily check writes euvd/stats.json, and the page draws from it.

This page is pre-rendered: the charts are drawn into the file, so it needs no JavaScript. Hover a bar or dot for its value where your viewer supports it.

Part 1: The EUVD as a whole

All 401,624 records, not only the exploited ones. Monthly counts come from /api/search with fromDate/toDate (publication date) and the assigner, fromScore and exploited filters; the response field total gives the count without downloading the records.

Volume1. Records published per month

/api/search totals per month, October 2024 to 7 October 2026 (documented filters).

What you seeEach bar is the number of records the EUVD published that month; the darker part is the critical ones (CVSS 9 or more). Volume ran at about 4,000 a month until the end of 2025 and then climbed to about 15,000 in September 2026. Critical records grew with it, from about 300 to 1,500 to 1,800 a month.

Limits: The date is when the EUVD published the record, not when the vulnerability was found. A jump can mean the EUVD ingested a new source rather than that more vulnerabilities exist; the API does not say which. October 2026 has one week.

Part 2: Actively exploited vulnerabilities

The EUVD entries flagged as exploited, the KEV catalogues behind them, and the honeypot sensors.

Time series5. Known-exploited additions per month

/api/kev/dump (documented). One entry per CVE with its earliest date across CISA KEV and EU KEV. Refreshed daily at 07:00 UTC by ENISA.

What you seeEach bar is one month since Nov 2021, split by which catalogue lists the CVE. The marked bar is September 2026, the month the SRP went live. CISA drives almost everything; the EU KEV adds a thin layer. The tall bars at the start are CISA building up its catalogue, not a surge, and the last bar (October) is only a week old.

Limits: The month of the earliest date, not the month of the latest listing. It says what is flagged as exploited, not how it reached ENISA. Nothing in this chart can show SRP reporting.

Time series6. Daily additions around the go-live

Same dump, by day, 15 Aug to 7 Oct 2026.

What you seeOne bar per day with at least one addition; the dashed line is 11 September, the SRP go-live. Most days without a bar are weekends.

Limits: A day without a bar is a day without additions, not missing data. Daily counts are small (0 to 9) and noisy; do not read a trend out of a few days.

Distribution7. How long after publication did exploitation start?

EUVD record: datePublished against exploitedSince for entries first flagged in the last 12 months (309 entries).

What you seeBars count entries by the gap between publication in the EUVD and the day the vulnerability was flagged as exploited. The tallest groups are same day and the first week, and a long tail runs past a year. The median gap is 13 days.

Limits: "Before publication" means flagged before the EUVD record existed, usually a record created late. The gap measures when it was flagged, not when exploitation began.

Comparison8. Who is faster: the EUVD or the KEV catalogues?

EUVD record datePublished against exploitedSince (the earliest KEV date), for every exploited entry flagged since January 2025 (507 entries), grouped by the month it was flagged. Months with fewer than 5 entries (October 2026 so far) are left out.

What you seeTop: for each month, how many entries were in the EUVD first, on the same day, or flagged by a KEV catalogue first. The EUVD record is usually older or the same age, and the catalogue is rarely first. Bottom: the gap in days. The bars are the median and the hollow dots the mean. Over the whole period the median is 15 days but the mean is 490, because a few entries sat in the EUVD for years before a catalogue flagged them; the median is the fairer "average". Since the go-live (29 entries) the median is 1 day and the mean 40, the shortest gaps in the series.

Limits: The gap runs from the EUVD record to the first KEV listing, so it says how long an entry waited in the EUVD before being flagged, not which source knew first: the vulnerability may have been public elsewhere earlier. The shorter gaps in September 2026 are an observation; they may be ordinary variation, and the chart cannot say why.

Honeypot9. Honeypot activity per entry: today against the usual level

/api/honeypotObservations/batch (undocumented, used by the EUVD frontend). Shadowserver honeypot sensors: connections in the last 24 h and averages over 7, 30 and 90 days.

What you seeEach row is one exploited vulnerability that sensors see attacked right now. The four dots, left to right on a logarithmic scale, are the 90-day, 30-day and 7-day averages and today (filled). A dot far right of the others is a surge. Red rows are the EUVD's own UPTICK flag; grey is steady; green is declining.

Limits: These are connection counts from sensors, not victims and not the reporting route. Only 330 of 1746 exploited entries appear in the sensors at all. A real time series needs a daily history that the EUVD does not keep (see panel 19).

Coverage10. How much of the exploited list do sensors see?

Same honeypot endpoint, checked for all 1,746 exploited entries, grouped by the year they were flagged.

What you seeBars show the share of entries per year that have honeypot data. Roughly one in five is visible to the sensors; 2026 has the lowest share (15%).

Limits: Absence from the sensors does not mean an entry is not attacked, only that Shadowserver does not emulate it. Edge devices and web applications dominate what sensors see.

Ranking11. Which vendors are flagged most often

EUVD records: vendor on each exploited entry, by exploitedSince; last 90 days against the 90 days before.

What you seePairs of bars per vendor: the last 90 days in blue, the 90 days before in grey. A vendor whose blue bar is much longer than its grey one is newly in the headlines (Fortinet, SonicWall). "n/a" is entries without a vendor name.

Limits: Counts are entries, not incidents, and one product family can contribute many CVEs. The EUVD has no vendor field in the KEV dump, so this needs the per-entry records.

Scatter12. Severity against likelihood of exploitation

EUVD records of the 50 entries flagged since 1 Sep 2026: CVSS base score and EPSS (the EUVD's own epss field).

What you seeEach dot is one newly flagged vulnerability. Horizontal: CVSS severity. Vertical: EPSS, the modelled chance of exploitation in the next 30 days. The lower right is where severity and EPSS disagree: critical flaws that the model rated unlikely, yet they are exploited.

Limits: EPSS is a prediction made before the fact. Entries here were already exploited, so low values show misses of the model, not errors in the data. EPSS changes daily; the chart would show the value at the last check.

Change13. How often do exploited entries change?

EUVD records: dateUpdated of the exploited entries, per day, last 30 days. Also available through fromUpdatedDate in /api/search.

What you seeEach bar is the number of exploited entries the EUVD edited that day (scores, references, advisories, product data). Edits come in bursts after a catalogue update.

Limits: This is editing activity on the record, not a change in whether something is exploited. A change in the exploitation status itself is only visible by comparing the daily snapshots the monitor keeps; the EUVD offers no history.

Comparison14. EU KEV against CISA: who lists a vulnerability first

/api/kevEntries/batch (undocumented): the day each catalogue added every entry the EU KEV lists. Shown: the 39 entries the EU KEV added since 1 July 2026.

What you seeOne thin row per entry, newest at the top. A gold dot is the EU KEV date and a blue dot the CISA date; the grey line joins them, and a triangle at the left edge means CISA added it before 1 July. Across all 60 entries in both catalogues the EU KEV mostly caught up on older CISA entries (before August 2026 CISA was first for 27 of 31). Since August the two are level: EU KEV first for 10, same day for 10, CISA first for 9. The median gap is 0 days since August (the mean is 68 days with CISA ahead, pulled by a few older entries); before August the median was 304 days with CISA ahead. Rows with only a gold dot are not in CISA's catalogue.

Limits: The EU KEV is not the SRP. Its dates go back to January 2025, and no entry carries an EU KEV date from 11 to 20 September 2026. An entry the EU KEV listed after the go-live and CISA did not list, or listed later, is a candidate for an SRP origin, nothing more. The date shown is the day the catalogue added the entry, not the day the vulnerability was reported.

Time series17. How urgently CISA wants new entries fixed

CISA KEV catalogue (known_exploited_vulnerabilities.json, schema fields dateAdded, dueDate, forensicTriage): entries added since January 2025, by month.

What you seeEach bar splits the month's new entries by the remediation window CISA set for US federal agencies: 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, 21 days. The dots count entries marked for forensic triage. Until February 2026 three weeks was the norm; in March and April it became two weeks, and from June three days dominates. Forensic triage appears from July 2026 and covers about three in four new entries since.

Limits: These are deadlines for US federal agencies, not for anyone in the EU, and they show how CISA rates urgency, not how dangerous a flaw is in Europe. The field forensicTriage refers to BOD 26-04 and does not exist for older entries.

Profile18. Ransomware use and weakness types

CISA KEV catalogue: knownRansomwareCampaignUse by year added, and the cwes field across all 1734 entries.

What you seeLeft: the share of entries CISA knows to be used by ransomware groups, by the year they were added: about one in four in 2021 and 2022, about one in ten in 2026. Right: the most frequent weakness types. Input validation, command injection and memory errors lead.

Limits: "Known" ransomware use is added as CISA learns of it, so recent years are low partly because the knowledge has not arrived yet. One entry can carry several weakness types, and older entries often carry none.
